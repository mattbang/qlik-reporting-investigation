# Investigation, learning and implementation

## The opportunity I identified

Recurring management presentations combined information from several Qlik applications. Rebuilding that package through regional spreadsheets and slide preparation duplicated effort and separated the report from its maintained sources. I initiated an investigation into whether Qlik could generate the required outputs using the applications and visualisations already in place.

My ambition was a reusable reporting approach: common presentation layouts, country- and division-specific versions, appropriate distribution, and a consistent basis for management discussion.

## Learning through working examples

I began with Tabular Reporting and taught myself the template, level, page and filtering mechanisms. By 28 June 2024 I had generated a first report and shared the template and lessons with Qlik colleagues. My notes covered setup difficulties, filter interactions, product-level selection, chart colours, hyperlinks and the difference between regional and country outputs.

I explored country- and division-specific reports and distribution lists. This required thinking beyond the appearance of a slide: which audience should receive which scope, which product groups should appear, and how the same template should produce different versions. Preserved templates, recipient-list files and system-issued outputs support this work. The public demonstration uses fictional substitutes for all of them.

## Choosing the approach against the requirement

Tabular was useful for structured, repeated outputs from one application. The broader management package needed information from multiple applications and a presentation-oriented output. I therefore investigated Application Automation and PowerPoint Subscriptions alongside Tabular.

On 9 July I set out that distinction to a senior stakeholder. On 10 July I documented presentation quality, multiple existing apps, scalability, maintenance and source ownership as requirements for a discussion with the central team. I did not simply ask for a new tool: I brought existing output examples and a defined problem to solve.

## Recreating the presentation language

I built cover, agenda, chapter and content sheets to reproduce the existing PowerPoint style. This involved a close visual comparison of title positions, margins, backgrounds, fonts and logo alignment. The retained comparison decks from 14, 16 and 18 August show the progression of the investigation and the alignment questions I documented.

My aim was near-pixel-perfect recreation. Some elements were close, while grid granularity, font availability and text-size controls left visible differences. I requested help with finer grid settings and documented the proposed changes. The portfolio therefore demonstrates attention to layout fidelity without claiming a measured pixel-perfect score.

## Consulting specialists to resolve uncertainty

I contacted Qlik, explained the multi-app reporting and presentation-format requirements, and brought the central team into the discussions. My 7 August meeting summary records feasibility confirmation subject to known limitations and further refinement.

The discussions helped me check whether my understanding of the tools was correct and whether remaining gaps required consulting work. I continued building the prototype myself. Later correspondence records the decision to decline the consulting offer and continue internally.

## Execution and recommendation

By August I had created a draft multi-app reporting approach and a reusable presentation-sheet example. I shared comparisons and proposed making those templates centrally available. Further requests addressed grid control, fonts, access and the mail connector. I continued exploring later PowerPoint subscription functionality in October.

The records support a completed investigation, hands-on prototypes and recommendations. They do not establish a completed global reporting service or successful delivery of every proposed automation path.

## What prevented wider adoption

My retrospective account is that a central operating model was missing. The proposed service crossed application boundaries, so a regional analyst’s access and mandate were insufficient to operate it broadly. No designated HQ owner took responsibility for the required reporting scope, access coordination and ongoing maintenance. Related senior-management reporting existed, but regional requirements were not taken on as a supported central service.

The lesson I would bring to a new employer is to develop the operating model alongside the prototype: name an accountable owner, agree the permitted scope across source applications, establish an authorisation model, and define who maintains templates and distribution. This means coordinated, governed access, not unrestricted administrator access.

## What a next employer gains

I can take an open-ended reporting problem, learn unfamiliar capabilities, produce testable examples, compare options against business requirements, consult specialists effectively and present a recommendation. I also recognise when adoption depends on organisational decisions that a technically capable prototype cannot resolve on its own.
